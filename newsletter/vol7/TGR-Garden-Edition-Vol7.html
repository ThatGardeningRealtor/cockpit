The Vegetable Garden, Fall Edition

A Ruby Crush tomato transplant, riding shotgun on the way home from class.

Last week's class brought in David Rodriguez, and if you've been around San Antonio gardening for any length of time, you already know the name. He's been Bexar County's AgriLife Extension horticulture agent since 2006 and hosts the Saturday morning Lawn and Garden show on WOAI 1200 AM, about as close to a hometown hero as this hobby gets around here. He walked us through the vegetable garden start to finish, and the big point up front was this: San Antonio can grow something nearly every month of the year if the timing is right. Summer crops need to be done by mid June before the heat takes over completely, then it's time to pull everything, rest the bed, and get ready for what's actually the bigger planting window of the year.

Fall is when the real vegetable action happens here. Broccoli, cauliflower, and leafy greens go in as transplants through the cooler months, onions get their start in November and December for the biggest bulbs, and garlic goes into the ground in October. For tomatoes, determinate varieties like Celebrity and Ruby Crush, the one riding home in the truck, are the better bet against Texas heat over the bigger indeterminate types.

And, as always, David reminded us more than once not to step on the garden beds. It's funny how passionate he gets about it, but now that we've learned so much about soil compaction and soil health, it actually makes a lot of sense. Most of the real magic happens below the ground, out of sight. We just see the other half.

The Rule That Stuck With Me

Warm season crops like okra, peas, cowpeas, and sweet potatoes need to be direct seeded by mid June. After that, the heat wins. Pull it, rest the bed, and get ready for the real fall planting window instead. We've got a good stash of okra and cowpea seeds at LRH3 right now if you want to get a head start on next year, thanks to Jorge, our fearless leader with GROW, the dementia-friendly gardening group that meets at SABOT on Wednesdays.
 
What's Growing at LRH3

Turk's Cap, Salvias, and a Shrimp Plant With a Mind of Its Own

Turk's cap putting on a show against the roofline this week.

The native bed is having one of those weeks where everything looks exactly the way it's supposed to. Turk's cap keeps throwing its twisted little pink blooms, the salvias haven't slowed down, and the beautyberry clusters are filling back in right on schedule. It's the kind of bed that mostly runs itself once it's established, which is the whole point of planting native in Texas. With all the hummingbirds around lately, I'd bet some of that turk's cap is getting pollinated by them more than the bumblebees, they can get into that curled bloom a lot better. Not totally sure though, just another garden mystery. Turk's cap seed pods are also one of my favorite things to snack on straight off the plant, but I actually held a few back this year to try starting from seed. Curious if they'll come up true and still bloom pink, or if I'm in for a surprise.

Then there's the shrimp plant. Anita stopped by LRH3 for a visit last week, and it was so nice catching up, especially since she showed up with a batch of shrimp plants that turned out to be a little group effort, a collaboration between a few different plant folks on the other side of town who all sent some along with her. We got to talking about the ones gifted to me last year by my neighbor Mrs. Rose, and I told her honestly, I figured mine hadn't made it through last year's freeze. Wouldn't you know, right after Anita left I turned around and Mrs. Rose's shrimp plant was peeking out the back of the planter with what looks like its first bloom of the season. It's grown a little lanky, so it'll probably need a move or a trim once it settles into the native bed for good. Thank you, Anita, and thank you to everyone across the LRH group who had a hand in those plants too. Y'all make this place what it is.
 
Still Trying

My First Queen of the Night Didn't Quite Make It

The Queen of the Night bud, folded over before it ever opened.

This was going to be my first Queen of the Night bloom, ever, and I was so excited about it. The bud folded over before it had the chance to open, so that flower never happened this time around.

For anyone who hasn't grown one, Queen of the Night (Epiphyllum oxypetalum) spends most of the year looking like an unassuming leafy houseplant, then puts out a flower that opens after dark, fills the air with a strong sweet fragrance, and closes again by morning, gone in a matter of hours. It's an easy plant to root from a single leaf cutting, which is exactly how most people end up with one in the first place. Mine's the same way, and there's plenty to root up if anyone wants a start of their own. The plant's still here and still trying, so there'll be another shot at an actual bloom before the season's done.

If you want the real crash course on these, our own Queen of the Night queen, Jowan, put together a full masterclass on growing and blooming them, well worth the watch if you want to actually get one to flower: watch it here.
 
Creepy Crawlers

Working Late on the Job

Caught wandering the driveway, a good ways from the beds.

I was out there putzing around, just checking on things, when I noticed him crawling across the driveway. He climbed right up my hand, and we sat there checking each other out for a minute before I carried him over to one of the hidden brush pile gardens to grub out and do his thing. With all the birds and lizards roaming around, he was lucky to make it back into the garden in one piece.

Best guess based on the size and coloring is some kind of cutworm, but I'm honestly not confident enough in that to call it settled. If you know what he is, or what moth or butterfly he turns into, reply and let us know. Always curious to actually get these right instead of just guessing.

Funny thing looking at him up close, the front end looked downright peculiar, almost like a giant maggot, but the back end looked more like a little face, googly eyes and all. That's actually a classic caterpillar move, a false face on the tail end to fool predators into striking the wrong side, so the real head gets away clean.
