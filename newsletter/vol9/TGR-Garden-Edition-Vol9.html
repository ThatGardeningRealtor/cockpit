Bugs: The Good, the Bad, and the Glowing

Molly's specimen boxes. Half the class was learning who's who.

Molly is the integrated pest management specialist with Texas A&M AgriLife here in Bexar County, and she opened with a number that stopped me cold. About 95 percent of the insects in your yard are helping or harmless. Only 5 percent or less are the actual troublemakers. The rest are pollinators, predators, and the cleanup crew breaking down everything that drops in the garden. So when you see a hole in a leaf and reach for a spray that wipes out everything, you're mostly taking out the good guys that were keeping the bad ones in check.

So who are the good guys, and who's actually causing trouble?

 

Ladybugs, lacewings, and praying mantises
The good guys. They hunt the little sap suckers for you, for free. Molly's quick tell for true bugs: a short mouthpart usually means a predator, a long one usually means a plant sucker.

Leaf footed bugs
Not on our side. They go through five stages as they grow, and the babies do the most damage. Catch them in the first one or two by knocking them off into a bowl of soapy water.

Squash vine borers
Not on our side either. The adult is a moth that looks like a wasp. Pheromone lures that smell like the females will pull them right into a sticky trap.

The tip I'm trying first: tomato hornworms glow under a blacklight. Grab a cheap UV flashlight, walk the tomatoes after dark, and pick them right off. Molly also told us about a trial at a children's garden where picking cabbage loopers by hand worked just as well as spraying. Sometimes the old school way is the best way.

Cosmos blooming right through a pile of fall trimmings. Messy on purpose.

And one for fall cleanup. A single pair of chickadees needs 6,000 to 9,000 caterpillars to raise one brood, and a lot of next year's pollinators spend the winter tucked in the soil or hanging as a chrysalis. Rake every bed bare and you haul them off with the leaves. So leave at least one corner messy until spring. Like Molly said, you're not procrastinating, you're supporting the cycles. That's the best excuse for skipping yard work I've heard all year.

 

Try This Today

Next time you see damage, look before you spray. Flip the leaf, find who's actually there, and ask if it's one of the 5 percent. Half the time, it's a good guy already on the job.

Roselle Season, and a Bean That Won't Quit

Roselle blooming at LRH3. The tea comes later.

The roselle at LRH3 finally opened up, and it's a pretty one. Roselle is a hibiscus, kin to okra, and the part you're after isn't the flower. Once the petals wilt, the bright red, fleshy cup left underneath (the calyx) swells up over a few weeks. That's your hibiscus tea. Tart, bright red, and really good iced.

 

How to harvest roselle
Pick the red calyxes while they're still plump and juicy, a few weeks after the bloom drops. Cut the red part away from the green seed pod in the middle.

How to make the tea
Steep the fresh calyxes in boiling water for about 15 minutes. Honey, a little ginger, and a squeeze of lime take the edge off. You can also dry them and save them for later.

A yard long bean at the Children's Vegetable Garden at SABOT.

While I was over at the San Antonio Botanical Garden, I spotted this yard long bean climbing a trellis in the Children's Vegetable Garden. The pods really can stretch close to a yard, and the vines keep going right through our heat, long after regular green beans have given up for the summer. The trick is to pick them while they're still slim and snappy, about pencil thick, because once they swell up they turn tough and stringy. Seeing it growing there made me smile, because little ones have been learning to grow their own food in that garden since 1983. It's one of the oldest youth vegetable teaching gardens in the country, and it's right here in our backyard.
 
Real Estate Corner

Room to Breathe, in the Garden and the Market

Fall is when San Antonio gardens finally get a breather after the summer heat, and it turns out the housing market is catching its breath too. A few of you have asked me over the fence what homes are doing lately, so here's what the San Antonio Board of REALTORS® reported for September, in plain English:

 

Median price: $302,999
Down about 2 percent from last September.

88 days on market, on average
Up 11 percent from a year ago. Homes are taking longer to sell.

About 6 months of inventory
5.95 months, with 17,155 active listings. That's a lot of choices.

92.4 percent of original list price
That's what sellers got on average, measured against where they started.

If you're shopping, that means you can slow down, look around, and ask for things like help with closing costs or repairs. Please don't skip the inspection just to make an offer look stronger. If you're thinking about selling, pricing it right from day one matters more than ever, since that 92.4 percent mostly comes from homes that started too optimistic and had to come down. And if you're eyeing a spring sale, plant your beds this fall so they're filled in by the time the photos get taken.

October Checklist

Your October To Do List

Speaking of fall planting, next to April, October might be the best month to garden in San Antonio. Our first frost usually doesn't show up until late November, so there's still plenty of runway. Here's what's on my list:

 

Sow wildflower seed
Larkspur, poppies, and nigella. They sprout over winter and bloom in spring.

Plant the cool season garden
Lettuce, spinach, chard, collards, radishes, turnips, beets, and carrots from seed. Broccoli, cabbage, and cauliflower from transplants.

Garlic and onions
Garlic cloves go in now. Old timers plant 1015 onion seed on, fittingly, October 15.

Divide and share
Daylilies, iris, phlox, coneflowers, and daisies can be split now. Bring the extras to the swap.

Plant trees and shrubs
Fall is the best time. The roots settle in all winter before next summer's heat.

Dig the sweet potatoes
Get them up while the soil is still warm, then let them cure before you store them.

Sources: Texas A&M AgriLife Bexar County Horticulture and the Bexar County Master Gardeners.

Turfgrass: Picking the Right Lawn for Your Yard

Next week is lawns, straight from Dr. Emanuel Chavarria with Texas A&M AgriLife. A few things I didn't know: there's no St. Augustine seed for sale, so every St. Augustine lawn starts from sod or plugs. Bermuda wants more than 8 hours of sun, or it thins out and dies in the shade. And there's a dead simple catch can test that shows how much water your sprinklers really put down. Good timing, too, since it's about time to dial the sprinklers back for winter.
