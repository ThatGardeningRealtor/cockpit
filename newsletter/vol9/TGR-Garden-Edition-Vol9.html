Bugs: The Good, the Bad, and the Glowing

From Molly's specimen boxes. Know your good guys from your bad guys.

This class came from Molly Keck, the integrated pest management specialist with Texas A&M AgriLife here in Bexar County, and it changed how I look at every leaf with a hole in it. Her big number: about 95 percent of insects are beneficial or neutral, and 5 percent or less are the troublemakers. They're the predators, the pollinators, and the recyclers breaking down everything that falls in the garden. Knock everybody out with a spray and you lose the good bugs that keep the bad ones in check.

 

On our side: assassin bugs
Right up there with ladybugs, lacewings, and praying mantises, hunting the little stuff for you. Molly's quick trick for true bugs: a short mouthpart usually means a good guy, a long one means a plant sucker.

Not on our side: leaf footed bugs
Most go through five molts, and the babies are the biggest issue. Get them early, in the first one or two, by shaking them off into a pan or bowl of alcohol.

Not on our side: squash vine borers
The adult is a waspy looking moth. Pheromone lures that smell like the females can pull them into a sticky trap.

The part that stuck with me most: integrated pest management doesn't mean zero pests. You need a balance of insects for the whole cycle to work. Molly told us about a trial at a children's garden where hand picking cabbage loopers did just as well as spraying. And if you want a fun way to hand pick, tomato hornworms glow under a blacklight, and so do some other pests. Grab a UV flashlight after dark, shine, and pick.

One more for fall: a pair of chickadees needs 6,000 to 9,000 caterpillars to raise one brood. Raking every bed clean can haul off next year's pollinators too, since a lot of them overwinter in the soil or as a chrysalis. Leave at least a small area messy until spring. As Molly put it, you're not procrastinating, you're supporting the cycles.

 
October Checklist

What to Do in the Garden Right Now

Next to April, October is about the best month to garden in San Antonio. We're in Zones 8b to 9a, and our typical first frost doesn't show up until around late November, so there's real runway left. Here's what's on my list:

 

Sow wildflower seed now
Larkspur, poppies, and nigella go in this month so they get a few weeks of cool weather. They'll sprout over winter and bloom in spring.

Plant the cool season garden
Lettuce, spinach, chard, collards, mustard, radishes, turnips, beets, and carrots. Use transplants for broccoli, cabbage, and cauliflower.

Garlic and onions
Plant garlic cloves and onions now. Old timers plant 1015 onion seed on, fittingly, October 15.

Divide and share
Daylilies, iris, phlox, coneflowers, and daisies can be split and replanted this month. Perfect timing for a swap.

Plant trees and shrubs
Fall is the best time. Roots settle in over winter before next summer's heat arrives.

Dig the sweet potatoes
Get them up while the soil is still warm, then let them cure before storing.

Sources: Texas A&M AgriLife Bexar County Horticulture and the Bexar County Master Gardeners.

 
Real Estate Corner

September's Numbers: More Choices, More Time

The San Antonio Board of REALTORS® released its September report last week, and the theme is breathing room for buyers:

 

Median price: $302,999
Down about 2 percent from September 2025. The average was $363,341.

2,793 homes sold
Just under the 2,823 sold the same month last year.

88 days on market, on average
Up 11 percent from a year ago. Homes are taking longer to sell.

About 6 months of inventory
5.95 months, with 17,155 active listings. Sellers closed at 92.4 percent of original list price on average.

What that means in plain English: buyers have more homes to look at and more room to negotiate, and sellers who price right and show well from day one are the ones still moving. Remember, that 92.4 percent is measured against the original list price, so a lot of that gap is homes that started too high and had to come down. If you're thinking about listing in spring, fall is when the curb appeal work pays off. Plant now, and it's filled in by listing photos.

Source: SABOR September 2026 San Antonio Area Market Report, released Oct. 9, 2026. Metro wide figures; your neighborhood and price range can look very different.

Roselle Tea in the Making and a Bean That Won't Quit

A yard long bean at the Children's Vegetable Garden at SABOT.

Roselle blooming at LRH3.

That pink beauty is roselle, a cousin of okra and hibiscus, blooming right here at LRH3. Once the petals drop, the bright red cup underneath (the calyx) is what you steep for that tart hibiscus tea. The bean is a yard long bean I spotted at the Children's Vegetable Garden at SABOT, the San Antonio Botanical Garden. Fun fact: little ones have been growing veggies in that garden since 1983, and it's one of the oldest youth vegetable teaching gardens in the country.

Turfgrass: Picking the Right Lawn for Your Yard

Next class was all about turfgrass with Dr. Emanuel Chavarria from Texas A&M. A few things I didn't know: there's no St. Augustine seed for sale, so every St. Augustine lawn starts from sod or plugs. And there's a tool that tells you how much to water based on the actual weather, plus a simple catch can test to find out how much your sprinklers really put down.
