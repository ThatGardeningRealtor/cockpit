Master Gardener Intern

Copy What Already Works

Native grasses and coneflower at the Botanical Garden.

Dr. Barron Rector is a range specialist with Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, and his big idea is simple: copy what was already here and working. Before the lawns and the fences, early travelers wrote about Texas prairie grass as high as a horse's stirrup. Nobody watered it. Nobody fertilized it. It just fit.

 

Change is normal
Texas was once under a shallow sea, and just 10,000 years ago the Edwards Plateau was a spruce and fir forest. Landscapes change. The question is whether we work with that or fight it.

Everything goes downstream
Whatever we spray, dump, or wash off the driveway keeps going. Think one step past the problem in front of you.

You're the owner
His last point stuck with me. If you own a piece of land, even a small yard, you're the one looking after it. That's a good thing.

Fighting the land

Clear it, replace it, water it, spray it. Repeat every season.

Copying the land

Plants that belong here, fed by the rain you already get.

Want a starting list? My Best Native Plants for San Antonio guide covers what grows here without a fight.

He also read us an Aldo Leopold line I wrote down word for word: "There are some who can live without wild things, and some who cannot." I'm pretty sure you're in the second group.

And if you're house hunting, take the same walk at a showing. What's growing wild around the lot tells you what the soil, sun, and water can really support.

 

Try This Today

Next time you're out on a walk, look for what's growing on its own in an empty lot or along a creek. That's your cheat sheet for what will thrive in your yard with the least help.
 

Source: Master Gardener class with Dr. Barron Rector, Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, October 8, 2026.
 
Fall Garden

Still Growing, Just Cooling Off

Fresh fall beds, seedlings already up.

Cooler nights are a gift to a San Antonio garden. In summer the nights stay almost as hot as the days, so plants never get a break. Now they finally rest, and a lot of fall crops taste better than their spring versions. Here's what the Bexar County Master Gardener planting calendar says to put in the ground in November:

 

Transplants
Broccoli, celery, and spinach.

Seeds
Carrots, plus radishes through about November 25.

Onions
Sets go in starting November 15.

Strawberries
Plant crowns now for spring berries.

Winter color
Pansies, snapdragons, alyssum, dianthus, and calendula.

Source: Bexar County Master Gardeners planting calendar.

Real Estate Corner

Can a Mobile Home on Your Land Be Real Property in Texas?

Yes, it can. If more room to grow means land for you, this one matters. In Texas, a manufactured home on land you own can be treated as real property once a Statement of Ownership from the state housing agency (TDHCA) is recorded with the county.

 

Real property or personal property?
Lenders care a lot about this. The owner has to elect real property on the application and then record the paperwork with the county.

How it sits on the land
The foundation and how the home is anchored can affect financing and insurance, so ask early.

What's under and around it
Out in the country, check the well, septic, and electric. And yes, I'll check the soil too.

Right Plant, Right Place

Check the Rain Before You Buy the Plant

Flip the tag before you buy.

One more thing from Dr. Rector's class that I wrote down twice: Texas is so big that the rain changes a lot from one end to the other. Beaumont gets about 60 inches a year. Parts of Presidio County get about 4. San Antonio sits in between, at roughly 30, and some years that's feast or famine.

His advice: check what a plant needs for your area before you put any money or time into it. A plant that wants 60 inches of rain isn't going to be thrilled with our 30. Lynn Cox had a good companion tip in her class: hardiness zones only tell you how cold a plant can take, not how it handles our summer heat, so check that too.

A good example is the little gem magnolia that came up on our field trip. It's the compact cousin of the big southern magnolia, which can top 50 feet. Little gem is also evergreen, so it stays green all winter, and it usually tops out around 15 to 25 feet tall, sometimes taller. It can even bloom when it's only a few feet tall. It's a great pick for a smaller yard, as long as you plan around three things in San Antonio:

 

Water
It comes from the wetter Southeast. Water it deeply while it settles in, and give it a good soak during our hot, dry stretches.

Alkaline soil
Magnolias like acidic soil, and much of San Antonio sits on alkaline, limestone based soil. That can turn the leaves yellow with green veins, called iron chlorosis. A Texas A&M soil test tells you where you stand.

Afternoon shade
Morning sun with some afternoon shade helps. Strong light and heat make the iron problem worse.
 

Try This Today

Flip the tag before you buy. Look for the water need and the sun need, and ask yourself if your yard can really give it that. If it wants more rain than we get, plan on watering it a lot.
 

Sources: Master Gardener classes with Dr. Barron Rector (Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, October 8) and Lynn Cox; San Antonio rainfall average from my own native plant guide on getrealtytx.com. Little gem magnolia: University of Florida IFAS fact sheet ST 375, Texas SmartScape, USDA NRCS southern magnolia profile, and Bexar County Texas A&M AgriLife Extension on iron chlorosis and alkaline soils.
 
Early November Checklist

Before the First Freeze

 

Buy frost cloth now
Our first freeze can show up any time from mid November on. Get it before the stores run out.

Sow bluebonnets
November is the month. Pick a well drained spot with 8 to 10 hours of sun, and go easy on the water.

Water less, water early
Cooler days mean plants drink less. Water in the morning, at the soil, not the leaves.

Hold the pruning
Most pruning waits for winter, January to March, while plants are dormant. Spring blooming shrubs wait until after they bloom.

Sources: Bexar County Master Gardeners and Master Gardener classes with Texas A&M AgriLife.

Community Corner

Marigolds, Altars, and Free Trees

Art spotted at Muertos Fest.

Last weekend I went downtown to Muertos Fest at Hemisfair, San Antonio's free Día de los Muertos celebration with more than 80 handmade altars. The gardener in me noticed the flowers first. Those bright orange and yellow blooms are marigolds, called cempasúchil, and their color and scent are said to help guide spirits home. Día de los Muertos is this Sunday and Monday, November 1 and 2. Want your own marigolds next year? They're easy from seed and they love full sun.

That's my wife Lauren getting her sparkle on at Muertos Fest. She's a huge part of all of this, mostly behind the scenes, and I'm grateful for her every day.

And if you've been waiting for a sign to plant a tree, this is it. The City is giving away more than 1,000 free shade and ornamental trees at Día del Árbol, first come, first served.
