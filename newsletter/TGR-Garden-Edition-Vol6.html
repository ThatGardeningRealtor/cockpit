Master Gardener Intern, Class 3

Fruit Trees: Site, Soil, and the Fertilizer Schedule That Actually Works

Lunch break: a PB&J and a bowl of homemade cinnamon-lemon puppy chow a classmate brought to share.

Class 3 brought in Dr. Larry Stein to cover fruit trees start to finish, and the big takeaway up front was this: any fruit or crop is growable in Texas, it just depends on how hard you want to work for it. Before planting anything, though, it starts with a real drainage test, digging a post hole eight to ten inches wide and three feet deep, filling it with water, and watching how long it takes to drain. Twelve hours is ideal. If it takes six to eight days, skip the tree and build a raised bed instead.

Planting itself comes down to a few rules worth remembering: dig a hole the same size as the container, not bigger, soak the roots for an hour first, and drop the same soil back in, rocks and all, with no amendments or fertilizer mixed in. Set the tree at the same depth it grew in the nursery, water it in, then cut the top back to eighteen inches. That cutback sounds harsh, but it's what pushes the tree to send energy into new roots instead of struggling to support top growth it isn't ready for yet.

The Number That Stuck With Me

Ideal soil should sit around 5% organic material. Bexar County soil averages just 0.5%. Composting is how you actually close that gap, and the ratio to remember is 2/3 browns to 1/3 greens, working toward a 30-to-1 carbon-to-nitrogen mix.

Pro tip: soak your browns first, it helps kick-start the whole pile.
 
New In The Tank

The Guppy Tank Just Got a Green Team

Dwarf hair grass and a few sword plants, freshly added to the guppy tank.

Truth be told, the indoor shelf ran out of room, so a few plants got relocated to the guppy tank whether they liked it or not. In all honesty, they're doing great over there, already multiplying. Picked up some dwarf hair grass and a few different types of sword plants to help balance things out. The tank's been running a little nutrient-heavy lately, and rather than reach for a harsh fix, the plan is to let the plants and snails do the work, some simple filtration paired with a little patience while the whole system finds its balance.
 
Multiply and Divide

The Paviso Verde Just Said It's Time

A new aerial root pushing through the node on the paviso verde philodendron.

The whole indoor collection, plants saved along the way or handed off by someone else, has finally grown up. It's big enough now to actually propagate and share instead of just working to keep everything alive. This paviso verde philodendron is the clearest sign of it, pushing a brand new aerial root right out through the node.

The wild part is watching the tiny new growth break through that outer skin at the node, the same skin that holds in natural moisture and feeds it to the root as it pushes out, giving the new root system an actual shot at survival before it ever touches soil or water. Nature figured that out on its own. That's the kind of detail that makes this hobby worth it.

Circle of Life

A Hawk Was Watching Too

A young Cooper's Hawk, perched and watching, right around the same brush pile.

Caught a local hawk swooping into one of the brush piles this week, stalking one of the younger, tweenage-looking squirrels. Pretty sure it's a Cooper's Hawk, likely offspring from last year's nest high up in the pecan trees on the next street over. Those brush piles get stacked on purpose, leftover branches and trimmings left in a corner for critters and bugs to have somewhere to hide. Turns out that goes both ways. Whatever the squirrels take from the garden, something bigger out there is keeping its own tally.

Real Estate Corner

Right Tree, Right Place

The right tree, in the right spot, genuinely adds value to a home: shade that cuts cooling costs, real curb appeal, a yard that feels established instead of bare.

The wrong species, or the right one in the wrong spot, can turn into one of the costliest mistakes tied to one of the biggest investments you'll ever make. Right plant, right place, that's the rule, unless you're determined enough to make it work anyway.

Funny enough, "money doesn't grow on trees" dates back to at least 1891, first showing up in print in a North Carolina newspaper. Nobody's ever traced who actually said it first. Plant the right one in the right place, though, and it's about as close to money on a tree as real estate gets.

Picking Your Money Tree

Who: anyone planning to stay put a few years, this is a long-game payoff.

What: native, well-adapted shade trees, live oak, chinquapin oak, Texas red oak, hold value and don't fight the climate.

When: fall through early winter, roots settle in before summer heat hits.

Where: 15 to 20 feet from the foundation at minimum, more for the big shade trees, clear of septic and utility lines.

How: same rule from Class 3, hole the same size as the root ball, no amendments, water deep and consistent the first two years.
