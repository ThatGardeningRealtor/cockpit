Master Gardener Intern

Plant Pathology, or How to Play Plant Doctor

Class field trip to the Botanical Garden. Never gets old.

This class was plant pathology, which is really just learning to be a plant doctor. When something in the garden starts looking rough, the instinct is to grab a spray and hope. Most of the session was about slowing down and figuring out what's actually going on first, because a fungus, a bacteria, a virus, and plain old overwatering can all look an awful lot alike from the porch.

The trick that stuck with me most doesn't cost a thing. When a plant wilts fast even though the soil is still moist, cut a fresh piece of stem right near the soil line, drop the cut end into a clear glass of water, and just watch for a few minutes. If milky, smoky looking threads start streaming out, that's bacteria pouring out of the plant's plumbing, and you're looking at bacterial wilt. Tomatoes, peppers, and eggplant are the usual suspects around here. There's no bringing a plant back once it has it, so pull it, bag it, keep it out of the compost pile, and give that spot a break from the same plant family for a while.

 

The Seed Packet Myth

That "non-GMO" label on garden seed packets? It's mostly marketing. With only rare exceptions, the vegetable and flower seed sold to home gardeners was never genetically engineered in the first place, so the label isn't telling you much. Shop for the variety, the days to harvest, and what actually grows well in San Antonio, and let the sticker be a sticker.
 

One more plant doctor topic worth knowing if you own, or are shopping for, a home with big live oaks. Oak wilt is a fungal disease that has taken out a lot of live oaks across Central Texas. It spreads tree to tree through connected roots and on little beetles drawn to fresh cuts. The habits that protect your trees are simple: skip pruning oaks from February through June, and paint every cut right away. If you're house hunting and notice dead or dying oaks up and down a street, that's worth asking about before you fall in love with the shade. Big healthy oaks are one of the first things buyers fall for around here, so they're worth protecting.

The Garden's Night Shift

Moonflowers still holding their shape the morning after, dew and all.

Some of the best stuff in the garden happens after I've gone to bed. The moonflowers, which is what most folks around here call datura, open their big white trumpets in the evening and hold them into the cool of the next morning, still beaded with dew when I made my rounds. They're built for night visitors. Pale blooms practically glow in the moonlight, and their sweet fragrance helps sphinx moths find them in the dark. One important note if you've got kids or pets: every part of datura is poisonous, so this is a look but don't taste kind of plant.

The morning after a one night bloom.

Then there's this one. A cactus staked up against the side of the house pushed out a bloom overnight, and by the time I caught it the next morning it was already starting to fold back up. Night blooming cactus flowers are like that, all that effort for a few hours in the dark, timed for the moths making their rounds. After last month's Queen of the Night heartbreak, I'll take it.
 
Real Estate Corner

Texas Natives Don't Mess Around

Think about what a plant has to survive to call San Antonio home. Triple digit summers, months without rain, then a gully washer that floods the yard, all on top of rocky caliche soil that would make most nursery plants give up. Texas natives have been handling all of that forever, and they make it look easy. A few of my favorites:

 

Texas Sage (Cenizo)
Silvery leaves that burst into purple bloom right after a rain, which is why folks call it the barometer bush.

Gulf Muhly
From mid September through November it puts up billowing pink clouds that glow in the afternoon light. It's peaking right now.

Turk's Cap
A hummingbird magnet that's happy in the shade under big live oaks, where most sun lovers give up.

Here's where it gets good for your home. Once they're established, natives can use up to 60 percent less water than a turf lawn, and buyers notice a yard that clearly takes care of itself. Fall is the best time to plant them here, so if a spring listing is on your radar, getting transplants in the ground now gives them a whole cool season to root in before listing photos. And if you're in an HOA, Texas law limits their ability to block drought tolerant landscaping, though they can still ask to approve your plans.

Want the full list? The Best Native Plants for San Antonio Yards guide on the blog covers more than a dozen natives that thrive here, the local nurseries that actually stock them, and a no dig way to swap out a thirsty lawn. Go find your new favorite.

Harvest Report

Better Late Than Never

Fashionably late, but they showed up.

We finally dug up the potatoes. I'm pretty sure we planted about twice as many as we pulled out, and truth be told, these should have come out of the ground months ago. Around here, spring potatoes go in late in the winter and are ready once the tops yellow and die back, usually by early summer. Leave them sitting in hot soil much longer than that and you start losing them to rot and whatever else is living down there. Lesson learned, and still a pretty good haul for a crop I'd half forgotten about. And if you're dreaming of a yard with room for a real potato patch, or a few acres for a whole lot more, that's a search I love running.
 
Unexpected Visitor

Somebody Crashed the Hummingbird Feeder

Not a hummingbird, and I'm not complaining.

I went to check on the hummingbird feeder and found a Baltimore oriole helping himself. Orioles have a serious sweet tooth, and fall is when they pass through Texas on the way south, so a sugar water pit stop isn't that unusual. Still, that black hood and bright orange belly were about the last thing I expected to see hanging off a feeder built for birds a fraction of his size. If you'd like to roll out the welcome mat, orange halves and a little grape jelly are classic oriole bait.

Bugs: The Good, the Bad, and the Glowing

From Molly's specimen boxes. The assassin bugs are on our side. The leaf footed bugs and squash vine borers, not so much.

Next week is all about insects, straight from Molly Keck, the integrated pest management specialist with Texas A&M AgriLife here in Bexar County. According to her, somewhere around 95 percent of the bugs in your yard are either helping or harmless. Tomato hornworms glow under a blacklight, which makes nighttime hand picking a lot more fun than it has any right to be. And a single pair of chickadees needs thousands of caterpillars to raise one brood, which might be the best excuse yet for leaving your fall cleanup a little messy.
