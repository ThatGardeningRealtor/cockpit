Seeds, Cuttings, and a Few Cheap Tricks

Mission control, where the propagating happens.

Our teacher has one big rule: know your plant. Does it grow from a cutting, a low branch, or a seed? Does the seed like light or dark? Last time we covered the setup. This time, here are the little tricks that save you money.

 

🥛 Do the float test
Soak your seeds in water first. Seeds that sink are good. Seeds that float may not grow. Don't leave them in too long.

🏷️ Clearance seed is fine
Seed packs have a date on them. Most seeds still grow after that date. So grab the cheap ones on sale.

🚰 Let tap water sit
Fill a jug and let it sit 2 to 3 days. That lets the chlorine leave the water.

🌱 Skip the fancy rooting gel
Rooting powder is just a strong dose of the stuff plants already use to grow roots. The cheap kind works fine. Woody stems need a little more.

🔥 Reusing old mix?
Put old soil in a pan, 4 inches deep. Cover it and bake it for 30 minutes at 180 to 200 degrees. That kills the bad stuff, and the good stuff too. Now it's clean, empty soil. That's fine for seeds, since a seed carries its own food to get started. When you move a plant to a bigger pot, or it stays in that pot a long time, mix in some compost or a little fertilizer.
 
 

❄️ The fridge trick

Convince a Seed That Winter Came

Some native seeds won't grow until they feel a cold winter. So fake one! Wrap the seeds in a damp paper towel. Put them in a bag. Leave them in the fridge for 30 to 60 days. Seeds with a hard shell, like bluebonnets, need a different trick. Rub them on sandpaper or nick them so water can get in.

 

✂️ Quick brief

Rooting Cuttings 101

1

Snip it right. Cut 4 to 6 inches off a healthy stem. Cut just below the spot where a leaf grows out. Roots will grow from there.

2

Straight into water. Put each cutting in water right away so it doesn't wilt.

3

Strip and dip. Pull off the bottom leaves. Dip the cut end in rooting powder.

4

Stick it. Push it into damp potting mix in a clean cup. Write the name on a tag in pencil.

5

Tent it and wait. Cover it with a clear bag. Keep it in bright light, but not hot sun. Wait a few weeks. Give it a soft tug. If it pulls back, it has roots!

Easy first tries: coleus, salvias, rosemary, and pothos.

My Fern Experiment

Step one: spore dots under a fern frond.

Step two: a green carpet. Fronds come later.

Remember the little brown dots under the fern leaf last time? Those hold the spores. Spores are like super tiny seeds for ferns. One leaf can hold thousands. Here's how we started them in class:

🌿 Fern spore checklist

✓

Step 1: Scrape
Hold the leaf over a sheet of white paper and scrape the dots off. The spores look like brown dust.

✓

Step 2: Save
Pour the dust into a small jar. Put a label on it. Use some now and save the rest.

✓

Step 3: Sprinkle
Shake a thin layer on top of damp, clean potting mix. Don't cover them with soil.

✓

Step 4: Seal
Zip the cup in a clear bag so it stays wet. Keep it in bright light, but out of hot sun.

First you get a green fuzz, like the example above. That's the baby stage. Tiny fern leaves pop up after that. I brought home three kinds of ferns. It takes about 12 weeks to see much, so stay tuned!

Sow It Now

Next Spring's Bluebonnets Go In This Month

Want Texas native wildflowers in March? Plant now. TxDOT says mid November is best. December 15 still works. The seeds grow roots all winter and bloom when it warms up. They can take 15 to 75 days to sprout, so be patient.

 

Seed to soil contact
No tilling. Scatter on bare ground, or mow low and rake out the thatch first. Cover with no more than a quarter inch of soil so the birds don't get it.

Water, then wait
Water it in, then give it a light drink about every three days for three weeks if it doesn't rain. Skip the fertilizer. You'll get leaves, not blooms.

Add a few friends
Indian blanket helps crowd out grass you don't want. Purple horsemint smells like lemons and brings in hummingbirds and butterflies.

Want more plants that love our heat? Check out the native plants guide on the blog. My Master Gardener class likes Native American Seed, out of Junction. And wildflower.org shows what grows where.

Sources: TxDOT Planting Bluebonnets; Master Gardener class on propagation, Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, October 29, 2026.

Know Your SAWS Watering Day

SAWS moved us back to Stage 3 on September 21. Sprinklers run once a week on your day, 5 to 10 AM or 9 PM to midnight, set by the last digit of your address: 0 or 1 Monday, 2 or 3 Tuesday, 4 or 5 Wednesday, 6 or 7 Thursday, 8 or 9 Friday. You can use a hose in your hand any time.
