Master Gardener Intern

Make More Plants for Free

Our instructor's one rule: know your plant. Does it root from a cutting, a low branch, or seed? Does the seed want light or dark? Answer that first and you stop wasting time. And if the tag says Unauthorized Propagation Prohibited, that plant is protected, so no making copies to sell.

 

Clean everything
Clean pots, a clean work area, fresh sterile mix, and clippers dipped in disinfectant between plants.

Skip the garden dirt
Try about half peat moss or coco coir and half perlite, vermiculite, or sand. No yard soil, compost, or fertilizer in the tray. Reusing mix? Bake it 4 inches deep, covered, for 30 minutes at 180 to 200 degrees.

Label in pencil
Ink fades. Write the plant and the start date, because one bump of the table can scramble a whole tray.
 
 

The fridge trick

Convince a Seed That Winter Came

Lots of native seeds won't sprout until they've been through a cold winter, so fake one. Wrap them in a damp paper towel, bag them, and leave them in the fridge for about 30 to 60 days. That's stratifying. Hard coated seeds like bluebonnets get scarified instead: nick or sand the coat so water can get in.

 
 

The lazy way

Root It Without Cutting It

My favorite part. With lantana and Turk's cap, skip the cuttings and try layering. Scrape a little bark at a node on a low branch, dust it with rooting hormone, and bury that spot just under the soil while it's still attached. Once it roots, snip it free. Easier than clipping and replanting.

My Fern Experiment

Sprouted fern spores, the example tray from our propagation class.

Remember the orange dots under that fern frond last issue? Those are spores, and this green carpet is what they look like once they sprout. Our class collected spores from different ferns, and I brought three kinds home. The catch: about 12 weeks before anything really happens. So we'll see.

Billy Bob, my very fluffy fern tray supervisor.

 

Try This Today

Find a lantana or Turk's cap branch near the ground. Scrape it, bury it, and set a rock on it. Check it in a few weeks.

Sow It Now

Next Spring's Bluebonnets Go In This Month

Bluebonnets in Boerne, just west of us. Photo: Carol M. Highsmith, Library of Congress, public domain.

The March show starts now. TxDOT says to get bluebonnet seed down by mid November for the best results, and as late as December 15 still works. The seeds sprout, build roots all winter, and bloom when it warms up. Germination can take 15 to 75 days, so be patient.

 

Seed to soil contact
No tilling. Scatter on bare ground, or mow low and rake out the thatch first. Cover with no more than a quarter inch of soil so the birds don't get it.

Water, then wait
Water it in, then give it a light drink about every three days for three weeks if it doesn't rain. Skip the fertilizer. You'll get leaves, not blooms.

Add a few friends
Indian blanket crowds out weeds, and purple horsemint smells like citrus and pulls in hummingbirds and butterflies.

Our class recommended Native American Seed and Wildseed Farms, and wildflower.org shows what grows where.

Sources: TxDOT Planting Bluebonnets; Master Gardener class on propagation, October 29, 2026.
 

Quick Local Take

Know Your SAWS Watering Day

SAWS moved us back to Stage 3 on September 21. Sprinklers run once a week on your day, 5 to 10 AM or 9 PM to midnight, set by the last digit of your address: 0 or 1 Monday, 2 or 3 Tuesday, 4 or 5 Wednesday, 6 or 7 Thursday, 8 or 9 Friday. A hand held hose is fine any time, which is good news for seed trays.

Real Estate Corner

Selling in Spring? Plant the Seeds This Winter

Same idea as those bluebonnets. March through June is peak listing season around here, and the homes that shine in spring did their prep over the winter. If you're even halfway thinking about it, here's what I'd do now:

 

Know your number
See what your home is worth, then let's fine tune it together. Texas is a non disclosure state, so online guesses can be way off.

Get ahead of the inspection
A pre listing inspection shows you what a buyer's inspector will find. Around here, think foundation movement on clay soil, a tired AC, or an old water heater.

Do the cheap stuff first
Fresh mulch, a clean entry, neutral paint, and a deep clean usually pay you back. A full kitchen remodel usually doesn't.
