Master Gardener Intern, Week 1

It All Starts With the Soil

Greenies, freshly tilled and ready for the next round of hands-on training.

This was officially week one as a Master Gardener intern, and it opened with a lesson on watering. Rather than let it sit as classroom theory, I took it straight into the garden that same weekend and deep watered our beds and trees the way we learned.

That same week we touched lightly on fertilizers and got a first real look at how differently soil can behave just a few feet apart in the same bed. There's a lot more to unpack there, and I'll be going deeper on soil types and what's actually happening underground in next week's issue.

Mulch That Actually Works

Start with a natural, beneficial compost as your base, then mulch beds and around trees while keeping it pulled back a few inches from the base of the plant rather than piled against the stem. Done this way, it holds moisture in while still letting the soil breathe, suppresses weeds, keeps the ground cooler, and lets water actually penetrate instead of running off the surface.

Bonus Round

GROW Just Wrapped Its First Week

Home to GROW, our dementia-friendly gardening group.

We just enjoyed week one this past Wednesday, and it's already clear this is going to be a good season. GROW is managed by SABOT and the Master Gardeners, working alongside volunteers to give people living with dementia and their caregivers real time in the garden together.

Beat the Birds to This One

Beauty Berry, Before the Beasts Got There First

Lucky to catch these before the birds did.

American beautyberry is one of those plants that just works here, producing mild, slightly sweet berries that the birds and critters chase after just as eagerly as I do. It holds up beautifully in Central and South Texas conditions without asking for much fuss in return, which makes it an easy plant to love.

Blooms are scattered all over the place right now too, some brand new, some old standbys, and a few already well past their prime. That layered mix is exactly what late summer looks like in a garden that's been left to do its thing.
 
Lost the Race Again

The Birds Beat Me to the Figs.

So We Planted More.

The birds got there first. The ants finished the job.

Birds are quick to test ripeness, and they've been beating me to nearly every fig this season before I get the chance. Rather than fight that losing battle, we planted a few more trees, figuring more trees would mean better odds and a few figs left over for us too.

On top of that, somebody quietly dropped off a few Celeste fig trees along with a batch of drought-tolerant natives, no note and no name attached. Whoever it was, thank you. They're perfect additions for Central and South Texas conditions, and that kind of quiet generosity is exactly the kind of thing that keeps happening around here.
 
Past Due

We're Overdue to Pull the Pumpkin Patch

Past its prime and past due.

Time to pull the pumpkin patch, plain and simple. These vines went for it this season, climbing up the house, up the fence, and across just about everything else in the garden that would hold still long enough. The seeds actually came from a member of the San Antonio Composting Project, so credit where it's due, that's about as loyal to the soil as it gets, literally their own motto. Besides the pumpkins, the tomatoes and squash need to come out too, and the black-eyed peas are ready for a dry harvest. Can't wait to get into more fun facts about legumes with everyone once those are in. All of that spent plant material isn't waste, it's the start of some genuinely great compost.

The bigger vision here is going all in on composting. A garden this size produces a serious amount of compostable organic material, and it only makes sense to put in the work to break it down and return it to the soil. That process feeds the bacteria, fungi, earthworms, and every other microorganism doing the quiet work of turning that material back into nutrients the plants can actually use. Bed prep, composting, and remulching form a cycle that never really stops, and it comes back to the same lesson running through everything else this week: get the soil right first, and the rest of it tends to follow.

Once they're out, it's straight into amending the soil and getting into the fall planting season. What should we grow next?
